Flow-field Mortality Forecaster
Samuel J. Clark · Research preview, November 2026 (replaces the April 2026 preview)

Modern mortality forecasting follows two main traditions descended from Lee & Carter's seminal 1992 paper [1]. One decomposes age-by-time mortality into a few summary patterns and projects those patterns forward. The other models how fast life expectancy improves at each level of life expectancy. We argue that both can be viewed as restricted versions of a single underlying object: a smooth trajectory through a low-dimensional summary of mortality derived from a multi-way decomposition of the sex × age × country × year mortality array. The unrestricted version of this object is the flow-field forecaster. We compare it with the leading existing methods on the Human Mortality Database [6]: each method sees only the data up to the year its forecast starts. The results so far are encouraging.

How we compare forecasts

For 43 populations in the HMD, forecasts start every five years from 1900 to 2015 and run 50 years ahead. Twelve forecasters compete. The flow-field is one. MDMx, a companion forecaster built on the same decomposition, Lee–Carter [1], Hyndman & Ullah's functional-data model [2], Hyndman, Booth & Yasmeen's coherent functional model [13], and the bayesLife/MortCast pipeline [3, 4] that the United Nations uses for its mortality forecasts [5] make ten more, each fitted both to all past years and to the last 50 (bayesLife from its own Bayesian estimation at every start year). An equal-weight average of the flow-field, MDMx and Lee–Carter is the twelfth. A random walk with drift is the baseline.

A comprehensive comparison scores each forecaster on five features: life expectancy for each sex, the life expectancy gap between the sexes, the age-specific differences between the sexes, the age pattern of mortality (weighted by where deaths occur, with life expectancy at 65), and the calibration of interval forecasts. Every score measures the size of the errors, relative to the baseline, so over- and under-predictions cannot offset each other. The forecasters are ranked over 20,000 random weightings of the five aspects, each with a resampling of the populations, so that no single choice of weights decides the result. The main test uses the forecasts from 1955–2000 at 1–15 years ahead. The forecasts from 2005–2015 were held out of every modelling choice and scored once, at the end.

What we're seeing so far

From 1955–2000, at 1–15 years ahead, the flow-field ranks first in 90% of the 20,000 draws, ahead of the ensemble average (7%) and MDMx. Its error in life expectancy is 0.42 years at 1–5 years ahead and 0.97 years at 11–15, against 0.48 and 1.55 for the baseline, and it is the only forecaster that beats the baseline on every one of the nine measures. Its lead is clearest beyond five years ahead; at 1–5 years the ensemble is as accurate. On the held-out forecasts from 2005–2015 the flow-field keeps its lead, with an error of 0.25 years at 1–5 years ahead. Forecasts from the early twentieth century, outside today's regime of mortality decline, are a harder test: there the flow-field still ranks first, but in fewer than half of the draws (47%, ahead of the ensemble's 29%).
